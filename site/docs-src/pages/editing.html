title: Editing text description: Moving, selecting, the clipboard, undo, indentation and line endings in ted. ---

Editing text

The editor works as you expect from any text field, with a few things of its own: indentation follows the file, and pasted text takes the file's line ending.

Moving and selecting

The arrows move by character and line, Ctrl with the left and right arrows by word (Option on macOS), Home and End to the start and end of the line (Home stops first after the indentation), Page Up and Page Down by a screen, and Ctrl Home and Ctrl End to the start and end of the file. Hold Shift with any of them to select. Ctrl A selects everything. Ctrl Backspace and Ctrl Delete delete the word before and after the cursor.

With the mouse: click to place the cursor, drag to select, Shift click to extend the selection, double click to select a word and triple click to select a line.

Context menu

A right click on the text opens a menu with Undo, Redo, Cut, Copy, Paste, Select all, Duplicate line and Delete line, each with its shortcut. A right click outside the selection moves the cursor there first, so the menu acts where the click was. The items that cannot act are greyed out: Cut and Copy without a selection, and the edits in a read only file.

Multiple cursors

Alt click (Option click on macOS) adds a cursor, and Alt click on a cursor removes it. Click with the middle button and drag to select a column: one cursor per line, each with the text between the columns where the drag started and where it is now. A line shorter than the column gets its cursor at its end. On Linux, the middle button does not paste the primary selection in the editor.

Typing, deleting and moving happen at every cursor, and undo takes them back at all of them in one step. Copy takes the text of every cursor, one per line. Paste gives one line to each cursor when the clipboard has as many lines as there are cursors, and the whole text to each one otherwise. Esc goes back to one cursor.

Alt J (Ctrl J on macOS) selects the word at the cursor. Pressed again, it adds a cursor on the next occurrence of the selected text, wrapping to the start of the file after the last one, as IntelliJ does. The match is exact: same case, any surrounding text. Alt Shift J (Ctrl Shift J on macOS) removes the last occurrence added.

Clipboard and undo

Ctrl C, Ctrl X and Ctrl V copy, cut and paste. With nothing selected, copy and cut take the whole line. Pasted text takes the line ending of the file. Ctrl Z undoes, and Ctrl Shift Z or Ctrl Y redoes; typing a word is one step.

Lines

Ctrl D (Cmd D on macOS) duplicates the line below itself and moves the cursor to the copy. With a selection inside a line, it duplicates the selection right after it and selects the copy; with a selection over several lines, it duplicates those whole lines. Ctrl Alt D (Cmd Alt D on macOS) deletes the whole lines of the cursor or the selection. With several cursors, both act at each one, as one undo step.

Alt ↑ and Alt ↓ (Option on macOS) move the line of the cursor one line up or down, and with a selection, the whole lines it touches. The cursor and the selection move with the text. With several cursors, every block of lines moves, as one undo step; nothing moves when a block is already at the start or the end of the file.

Ctrl L selects the whole line, and each press adds the next one. Ctrl Enter and Ctrl Shift Enter open a new line below or above the cursor's, with its indentation, wherever the cursor is in the line. Ctrl G asks for a line, or line:column, and goes there.

Ctrl / comments out the lines of the cursor or the selection with the comment of the language (// in Rust and JavaScript, # in shell, Python, TOML and Makefiles, <!-- --> around each line in HTML, XML and Markdown, /* */ in CSS), and uncomments them when they are all commented already. JSON, logs and plain text have no comments.

Some Linux desktops use Ctrl Alt D to show the desktop. There, the desktop takes the shortcut before ted sees it, and the shortcut has to be freed in the desktop's settings.

Word wrap

Long lines can wrap into several rows that fit the window, breaking after a space when they can. Markdown and plain text files wrap by default; code wraps when the Word wrap setting is on. Alt Z, or the Wrap toggle of the status bar, turns it on or off for the open file. While lines wrap, the arrows up and down move by rows, there is no horizontal scrolling, and the line number shows on the first row of each line.

Wrapping costs nothing on big files: ted only wraps the lines on screen. A line of more than 256 KB, such as a minified file, wraps every so many characters instead of at spaces.

Indentation

Tab indents and Shift Tab outdents: the cursor to the next stop, or every selected line by one level. Ctrl ] and Ctrl [ indent and outdent the whole line wherever the cursor is. The level follows the file: a file indented with tabs gets tabs, a file indented with two spaces gets two spaces. In a new or flat file, the Tab size and Insert spaces settings decide. The status bar shows what the file uses.

Line endings and encodings

ted keeps the line ending of each file, LF or CRLF, and the byte order mark of a UTF-8 file that has one. A file that is not valid UTF-8 opens read only, its unknown bytes shown as replacement characters, so saving can never change bytes ted could not read. Binary files are not shown.
